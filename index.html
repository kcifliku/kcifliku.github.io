A drone built in NJIT's Networking Research Laboratory to fly autonomous data-collection missions. I took it from component models to a flying aircraft, and I was responsible for everything that turns battery energy into thrust.

  • Modeled the airframe components in Creo Parametric, then fabricated the aircraft and ran flight tests to validate its performance.
  • Selected the motors, propellers, ESCs, battery and power distribution board as one system, sizing each against the thrust-to-weight target, the current each stage draws, and the pack voltage.
  • Ran eCalc and other propulsion simulations before committing to parts, confirming the motors, propellers, ESCs and battery were compatible with each other and with the quadcopter's layout.
  • Checked that every part of the power path stayed within its ratings so the aircraft operates safely and efficiently in flight.
  • Designed in room for full autonomy later: spare payload capacity and mounting space, and margin to upgrade the onboard processing.

A NASA-selected suborbital experiment from NJIT that measures electron density in the ionosphere with a pair of Langmuir probes. I worked on the electrical engineering team, testing the boards that power the payload and condition the probe signals.

The electronics are split across three boards, designed by teammate Nathanael Gunawan. Overwatch CEO carries the IMU, microSD logging, an XBee radio and the regulators. Scout ISO is a self-contained unit with its own Li-ion pack, an Arduino Nano 33 IoT and a second XBee. Scout BIAS is the analog board that sweeps the probes and reads their current through two SMA connectors.

  • Verified power integrity by instrumenting the 28 V to 5 V buck converters and the 5 V to 3.3 V low-dropout regulators, confirming stable rails across the payload.
  • Tested the triangular voltage generator that sweeps the bias on one probe pair, measuring a clean 10.4 V peak-to-peak (±5.2 V) triangle at 97.4 Hz on the oscilloscope.
  • Validated the input data of the transimpedance amplifier that converts probe current into a voltage the MCU can sample.
  • Brought up the boards on the bench and in their 3D-printed mounts before integration into the payload housing.

A charge controller that takes energy from a solar panel, steps it down to charge a 12 V battery at up to 10 A, and switches power out to a load. I split the design into three hierarchical schematic sheets, DC/DC, MCU and Power, tied together by a top-level sheet, then laid out the board in Altium.

  • Designed a synchronous buck power stage: two PSMN5R2-60YLX MOSFETs driven by an LM5109B half-bridge gate driver with a bootstrap supply, a 47 µH inductor, 1200 µF of input and 820 µF of output bulk capacitance, and a blade fuse on the battery output.
  • Measured charge and load current with 3 mΩ shunts feeding an INA2181 dual current-sense amplifier, with its reference set from the MCU's DAC. Resistor dividers report solar and battery voltage, and the MCU can switch the solar divider out.
  • Switched the load with a high-side P-MOSFET under MCU control, protected by an SMBJ24A TVS diode at the load terminal.
  • Generated the housekeeping rails: an LMR33630 buck makes 5 V for a USB-A charging port with ESD protection, and an AP2210 LDO makes 3.3 V for the logic, with a power-good signal back to the MCU.
  • Built the control side around an STM32L072: ADC feedback from the power stage, PWM to the gate driver, a UEXT header for UART, I²C and SPI, SWD programming, an RJ port carrying UART and an external temperature sensor, an onboard 10 k NTC for battery temperature, and five charlieplexed status LEDs on three pins.
  • Laid out the board with wide copper pours on the high-current paths, terminal blocks for solar, battery and load, and test points TP1–TP9 on the key nets for bring-up.

A board that takes a 5–30 V battery and splits it into six independent high-current outputs, each with its own fuse, while measuring the total current and voltage. I designed it in KiCad and had it fabricated.

  • Routed the XT60 battery input through automotive blade fuses to six separate high-current outputs.
  • Added current and voltage monitoring with an INA226 reading a 5 mΩ four-terminal shunt (WSL4026). Kelvin sense traces, I²C pull-ups and local decoupling keep the measurement clean.
  • Designed an LM2596 buck converter that holds a steady 5 V rail across the whole 5–30 V input range, choosing and validating the passives for efficiency, stability and thermal performance.

With my team, I designed and built a multistage audio system that captures a voice, cleans it up, changes it digitally, and plays it back through a speaker. The chain runs from an electret microphone through a pre-amplifier, a band-pass filter and an ESP32 modulation stage to a power amplifier, with an LED level display along the way.

  • Designed the pre-amplifier and band-pass filter around LM358 op-amps to lift the small, noisy microphone signal: a gain of about 100 and −3 dB cutoffs near 300 Hz and 3 kHz, the band that carries speech.
  • Designed an LM386 power amplifier with a gain of about 200 to drive the speaker from the modulated signal.
  • Modeled the stages in MATLAB and Multisim. Multisim has no microphone model, so we drove the input with an AC source and swept it across the frequencies the human voice produces to check gain and filtering.
  • Interfaced the analog front end with the ESP32, which applied the voice effects digitally: pitch shifting, a robot or ring-modulator voice, and echo.
  • Built a comparator-based LED meter with eight LM311s to show signal level.
  • Built the full system on breadboards, with a dual supply running the microphone at 5 V and the rest of the circuit at 9 V, then tested it stage by stage to track down noise and get a working end-to-end prototype.
